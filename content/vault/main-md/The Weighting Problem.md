# Naive Approach
So far, we have been working under the regime of index balance; in particular, our consistency requirements force $\max(\epsilon_{1}, \dots, \epsilon_{K}) \to 0$ for consistency of the $\hat{\gamma}$ solve, where we call $\epsilon_{k}$ as in page 6 of the supplemental an "index balance condition" (versus a "class" or maybe "dimension" balance condition, which we sometimes use to describe the event of large discrepancies in $\kappa_{k}$). But it seems as though in many of these Netflix-problem-esque person-product crosses, the requirement that all such $\epsilon_{k}$ go to zero is unreasonable. However, I do think it's reasonable that one or perhaps all but one $\epsilon_{k}$ go to zero. Put explicitly, it seems reasonable that in a sufficiently large user pool, each individual customer's contribution should be negligible, but that there may not be products that decay so nicely. If crosses describe grocery orders, for example, it may be reasonable to expect that eggs and milk constitute a nontrivial proportion of the observations. In something explicitly Netflix-esque, there are properties like the sitcom Friends or various children's media which we may expect have a longevity and draw that make their corresponding proportion observations asymtotpically nontrivial. I think the regime of having one class nontrivial is most natural, though maybe an "all-but-one" can have some natural meaning as well. 

When we loosen the index balance condition above, we need to consider a reweighting in the fit. Why? In the two crossed random effects case, we consider a toy model in which we align motivation with $a_{i}$ as a customer random effect and $b_{j}$ as a product random effect. Perhaps in the high data setting, we have 99% of data in the $b_{1}$ column. Though $b_{j}$ contribution is zero marginally, without the balance condition, a vast majority of our data is shifted by $b_{1}$, and thus our fit of $\beta$ will in turn be shifted; I believe that if $X_{i 1}$ is reasonably expressive in this setting, we'd recover something akin to an explicit shift in $\beta_{0} \leftarrow \beta_{0} + b_{j}$ if we borrow consistency arguments from previous iterations. 

So we need to reweight. Let us do this more generically; sticking to two random effects, let us have at least one asymptotically nontrivial weight among $\delta \in [0, 1]^{J} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{J}$  indexed by components $\delta_{j} = N_{\bullet j} / N$. The issue is this; optimal reweighting takes into account variance $\sigma^{2}_{B}$ and $\sigma^{2}_{A}$ to do some sort of column-level inverse-variance weighting. 

I think the natural naïve iteration is this; linearity gives consistency to the inverse proportion reweighting that makes column contributions equivalent. I can begin by fitting the log likelihood optimization of $\hat{\gamma}$ in the weighted regime,
$$\begin{aligned} \ell(\hat{\gamma}) = \sum_{j = 1}^{J}w_{j} \ell_{j \bullet} \end{aligned}$$
for $w_{j} = 1 / N_{\bullet j}$, which I reiterate nets a consistent estimator of $\hat{\gamma}$. Then this $\hat{\gamma}$ lets me solve for $\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{B}$ and $\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{A}$. In turn, I re-solve for $\hat{\gamma}$ with weights
$$\begin{aligned} w_{j} \propto \frac{1}{1 + (N_{\bullet j} - 1)\hat{\tau}^{2}_{B}}; \end{aligned}$$
it seems reasonable to assume a conversion of this kind of iterative process without much worry, though I don't know what theory would justify it.
