Here is a summary of progress generalizing ARC to other link functions. It does not include: 3 or more factors, random slopes, or replicates.  Motivation  We want scalable inferences for crossed random effects in generalized linear mixed models. The exact computations are intractable due to expensive algebra and high dimensional integrations. The current approaches include the Laplace approximation, penalized quasi-likelihood (PQL) and variational inference (VI). The Laplace approximation does not have theoretical support for the high scale problems we consider. Some very recent work by Reid and others gives sufficient conditions that are not satisfied in our motivating problems. It also shows some bias for the variance components. PQL makes a cascade of approximations and is known to be biased. VI makes such a radical change to the problem that it does not feel like a solution to the original problem formulation.  Logistic regression  If we just plug in the logistic link function into our prior ARC recipe we do not get a clear way to turn the resulting γ estimate into a β estimate. The solution is to fit the all likelihood with a random effect inside the link function. That gets us estimates of β and σ2 = σ2  A + σ2  B. This finding shows that the easy rescaling argument in the probit case is a consequence of the variance components not being identifiable in that case. (Or maybe just barely identifiable: this is to be checked.) The probit is degenerate or nearly so, and we exploited that. Once we have estimates of β and σ from the all log likelihood lall(β, σ2), we can write the row/column log likelihoods lrow and lcol in terms of one parameter like ρA = σ2  A/σ2. Some other computational strategies are described below. The optimization of these loglikelihoods is greatly enhanced by an approximation due to Monahan and Stefanski. They approximate the logistic CDF by a scale mixture of eight Gaussian CDFs. The method is accurate to within about 2 × 10−9. It would take an enourmous sample size of IID data to even notice that this was not the Gaussian. Compared to Laplace, PQL and VI, we can consider this to be exactly the logistic distribution. We lack a good numerical example. Many of the large scale data sets are intercept only. The logistic case is solved by PQL in a paper by Ghosh, Hastie and Owen. PQL is expected to have difficulties if many rows or columns have small sample sizes. It is also adversely affected by very skewed responses as arise for rare events. Examples like this would show the advantage of the new method.  Poisson loglikelihood  To grow from a logistic regression solution to a GLMM solution it is important to handle the Poisson log-likelihood case.  1
The logarithm of the Poisson probability can be approximated by adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature (AGHQ). Cristiano has an approximation in terms of a log gamma distribution that might work even better.  The regression case  For plain LLM (not generalized) with a Gaussian response, there is earlier work by Gao and Owen. That algorithm uses lall to get β. Then it uses the method of moments to estimate σ2  A, σ2  B and σ2  E. Then it fits a hierarchical model, like  either lrow or lcol to re-estimate β. Then it re-estimates σ2  A, σ2  B and σ2  E again by moments. The regression case may give the cleanest approach to compare fitting strategies in the section below. The Gao and Owen paper has a very detailed and complex description of the variance of the variance component estimates. Under mild sampling conditions those expressions simplify greatly. The paper by Ghosh, Hastie and Owen uses a backfitting (block coordinate descent) strategy to estimate the generalized least squares coefficients using both of the variance components. The algorithm has linear cost per iteration, and under some balance conditions, the number of iterations is O(1). The backfitting strategy is similar to alternating between lall + lrow and l + lcol. That is also analogous to collapsed Gibbs sampling.  Fitting strategies  There are several choices for the fitting strategy anchored in an understanding of composite likelihood. One approach is to optimize lrow + lcol over one scalar parameter after getting estimates of β and σ2 from lall. Another choice is to avoid the all likelihood and optimize lrow + lcol over all of the parameters. This is slower. Another choice is to use lrow + lcol − lall in case there is some redundancy in these loglikelihoods. This is similar (or more than similar) to the idea of using conditional pairwise likelihoods Pr(y | y′) instead of marginal pairwise likelihoods Pr(y, y′) which double count the marginal contributions Pr(y) and Pr(y′). Another choice is to optimize λlrow + (1 − λ)lcol in case the information content differs between those two sources in a way that we can exploit. A Poisson experiment with Claude (and AGHQ) found that optimizing lrow+ lcol over multiple parameters was slower than using lall plus a one dimensional optimization of lrow + lcol but gave very similar results. It also found for this example that lrow + lcol − lall had an indefinite Hessian.  Cauchy  A Cauchy link in a GLMM is called a cauchit model. It is implemented in glm in R. The Cauchy link gives some protection against label error (wrong Y values)  2
arising with small probability.
